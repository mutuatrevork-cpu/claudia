# More Than Technology — Accessible Transcript

**Claudia Casso | EDTC 6320**

Educational Technology is more than using digital tools. It is the professional work of understanding how people learn, identifying learning and performance needs, designing effective experiences, selecting appropriate resources, and evaluating whether those solutions work.

AECT describes Educational Technology as an ethical, research- and theory-informed field that uses strategic design, management, implementation, and evaluation to improve learning and performance. That means the profession is not defined by a particular device, app, or learning platform.

Educational Technologists work across the learning process. They analyze learners and contexts, identify gaps, develop learning experiences, create digital and multimedia resources, support accessibility, select technologies, and evaluate outcomes. They may develop online courses, employee training, performance-support resources, faculty development, onboarding, compliance learning, or technology-supported instruction.

The field also requires a broad professional skill set. Instructional design, learner analysis, e-learning development, multimedia design, accessibility, project management, communication, assessment, and evaluation are all common parts of the work. Technology matters, but the decision about which technology to use comes after the learning need is understood.

Educational Technology professionals work in many settings. UTRGV identifies K–12, higher education, corporate, healthcare, government, and military environments as examples. Job titles vary and may include Instructional Designer, Learning Experience Designer, E-Learning Developer, Educational Technology Specialist, Training and Development Specialist, Technology Integration Specialist, LMS Specialist, or Instructional Technology Coordinator.

There is not one single Bureau of Labor Statistics occupation called Educational Technologist, so salary information is best presented through related roles. In May 2025, the U.S. median annual wage was $69,280 for Training and Development Specialists, $77,440 for Instructional Coordinators, and $133,000 for Training and Development Managers. These figures are examples, not salary guarantees; earnings vary by role, industry, location, education, and experience.

The field also has a long history. Visual and audiovisual instruction expanded learning beyond print. Systematic training strengthened attention to objectives and performance. Learning theory and instructional design brought more deliberate methods for planning instruction. Personal computers, multimedia, the Internet, and online learning expanded access and interaction. Mobile learning, analytics, and artificial intelligence continue that evolution, but new technology does not replace the need for sound design.

Learning theory helps guide those design decisions. Behaviorism focuses on observable outcomes and reinforcement. Cognitivism focuses on memory, information processing, organization, and prior knowledge. Constructivism emphasizes active meaning-making, authentic problems, and experience. Multimedia-learning research and Universal Design for Learning also help Educational Technologists present information clearly and reduce barriers.

Professional standards provide another foundation. AECT standards address content knowledge, pedagogy, learning environments, professional practice, ethics, and research. In EDTC 6320, this project connects specifically to indicators 1.2, 1.5, 2.5, 3.1, 3.5, 4.1, 4.5, and 5.1.

Educational Technology is not simply about using technology. It is about understanding people, learning, and performance, then designing the right solution for the problem.
